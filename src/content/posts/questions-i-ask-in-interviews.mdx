For years I walked into interviews with a silent assumption: *they* were grading *me*. I prepped my answers, rehearsed how I'd tell the story of each project, worried about whether I was good enough. Then, at the end, the familiar line always showed up — *"Do you have any questions for us?"* — and I'd ask a couple out of politeness: what's the tech stack, how big is the team. Questions asked to look interested, not to learn anything real.

**Louise Deason**'s piece resets that whole frame. An interview isn't a one-way exam. It's two sides sizing each other up — and that "do you have any questions" moment is the rare stretch where the company *drops its guard*: it shifts from grading you to answering to you. Ask the right thing right then, and you pull out things no job posting or intro call will ever tell you.

<PullQuote>
You are not a variable waiting to be evaluated. You are the one doing the choosing — between options that are each flawed in their own particular way.
</PullQuote>

Deason boils it down to nine questions. After reading them, I sorted them under the four things I actually want to know before I say yes to a place.

## Do people here move up, or move out?

What a company *says* about growth is cheap. What's expensive is the evidence: when was the last time it *actually* happened.

<Finding n="01" title="&quot;When was the last time someone on this team was promoted? What did that process look like, and how long had they been here?&quot;">
You're probing whether the promotion path is transparent and whether it actually functions. A good answer is very specific: a name, a timeline, the criteria that were met. <InlineFlag>Red flag:</InlineFlag> vague talk about "review cycles," or an admission that nobody's been promoted lately — a sign the path upward is murky.
</Finding>

<Finding n="02" title="&quot;What's the longest anyone has stayed in this role, and what are they doing now?&quot;">
This exposes how stable the team is and whether there's room to grow. What's worth noticing is the difference: did the people who came before *move up* into management or a bigger role, or did they *leave*? Same number on the tenure clock, two completely different stories.
</Finding>

## Are they willing to be honest?

The next two questions don't just ask for the truth — they also measure whether the person across the table is willing to *tell* you the truth. And how they answer is a preview of the working relationship you're about to enter.

<Finding n="03" title="&quot;Is there something this team is genuinely bad at?&quot;">
Most candidates will get back a rehearsed humble-brag in disguise — the "we ship so fast our docs fall a bit behind" kind. A real answer names a real bottleneck, and shows whether this person is straight with you. <InlineFlag>Red flag:</InlineFlag> not being able to come up with a single weakness.
</Finding>

<Finding n="04" title="&quot;When the team disagrees with a decision from above, how does that play out?&quot;">
This one measures psychological safety — whether disagreement is genuinely welcome. <InlineFlag>Red flag:</InlineFlag> silence, "our team always agrees," or "we trust leadership" — all of which hint at either avoidance or groupthink. A strong answer describes an actual process: concerns get raised, decisions sometimes change, and there's always a reason attached.
</Finding>

## Is this chair steady?

Three questions I used to be most reluctant to ask, afraid they'd sound like I was doubting the place. But this is exactly the part that decides whether you're still sitting there twelve months later.

<Finding n="05" title="&quot;What's the team's headcount plan for the next twelve months?&quot;">
Is the team expanding, holding steady, or shrinking — and how safe is your role in that. One telling signal: "I'm not sure, that's being discussed at the leadership level" means the future of your position is still undecided, at a tier above even your soon-to-be direct manager.
</Finding>

<Finding n="06" title="&quot;When was the last reorg, and why?&quot;">
This exposes how unstable the organization is and how erratic the leadership layer can be. A reorg three months ago needs explaining. And the *reason* is what really weighs: a change of strategy is far from a change of leadership, and both are further still from a reorg driven by poor performance.
</Finding>

<Finding n="07" title="&quot;Why is this role open?&quot;">
Is this growth (new work), a backfill, or a reorg? <InlineFlag>Red flag:</InlineFlag> hesitation is data too. A pause before the answer is a cue to dig: "Did the previous person move internally or leave outright?" The answer to that tail end often decides whether the place is worth taking.
</Finding>

## Who will I work with, and what does done look like?

Finally, the everyday stuff: the people I'll sit next to, and the yardstick I'll be measured against.

<Finding n="08" title="&quot;Who would I work with most closely, and can I talk to them during the hiring process?&quot;">
This measures the team's transparency and how much confidence they have in their own people. Here's the interesting bit: the *friction* in the response tells you more than the content of the answer. Stalling, hard-to-schedule, or introducing you to the wrong person — all of it reveals gatekeeping habits.
</Finding>

<Finding n="09" title="&quot;Six months in, what does success look like in this role? And what does failure look like?&quot;">
Interviewers always have the first half ready, but few prepare for the "failure" half — so you get an unscripted, more honest answer. It reveals the kinds of failure the company has already tasted: the ambiguity you'll have to sit with, the politics, or the burnout risk baked into the role.
</Finding>

<Callout label="A few things I remind myself">
Don't ask about pay and comp with the CEO during the culture round, and don't touch benefits until you have an offer in hand — wrong timing is a bad signal about *you*. Watch for answers that *contradict each other across different interviewers*: that's a clearer red flag than any single question above. And if, over the whole session, they never leave you room to ask — that silence is itself the answer.
</Callout>

## Switching sides of the table

What I took away after reading isn't nine questions to memorize. It's a shift: from *trying to get chosen* to *doing the choosing*. Every company is flawed in its own particular way — these nine just help you learn early where the flaw is, so you can decide for yourself which flaw you can live with.

And there's a test even shorter than the nine questions: their attitude *while* you ask. A company that bristles at these has just handed you an extremely valuable piece of information cheaply — that it isn't worth your serious consideration. The trustworthy place is the one willing to sit down for a real, two-way conversation. Your leverage lives in noticing that.

<Aside>
I wrote this mostly to remind the me of five years ago — the one who sat there nodding along, afraid that asking too much would cost him points. Turns out asking the right question is exactly how you get your footing back.
</Aside>
